Fantasy points are a function of opportunity times efficiency, and the two are
not equally predictable. Opportunity is sticky. Efficiency regresses.

## Rank the signals

Roughly in order of how much they should move your opinion:

1. **Volume and role.** Targets, carries inside the 10, snap share, route
   participation. A player who is on the field on third down has a different
   job than one who is not.
2. **Team context.** Offensive pace, pass rate, and whether the offense is
   likely to be trailing. Bad teams throw.
3. **Draft capital and contract.** Investment predicts patience. Teams give
   opportunity to players they paid for, longer than the results justify.
4. **Age.** See the roster-construction article — age is the single best
   long-horizon predictor.
5. **Efficiency.** Yards per route, yards after contact, catch rate. Real, but
   noisy over small samples and prone to regression.

## The traps

- **Touchdown extrapolation.** Touchdown rate is the least stable component of
  fantasy scoring. A player scoring on 12% of his touches will not keep doing
  that, in either direction.
- **Three-game samples.** Almost every dynasty overpay starts with a hot
  stretch. If the role did not change, the production will not persist.
- **Narrative laundering.** "Breakout candidate" articles cite the same three
  numbers. Ask what changed about the role.
- **Confusing talent with situation.** A talented player behind an entrenched
  starter is a stash, not a starter. Both facts can be true.

## Archetypes that age differently

- **Pass-catching backs** hold value longer than early-down grinders, because
  receiving work does not depend on the legs holding up as long.
- **Contested-catch receivers** age better than separation-by-speed receivers.
- **Slot receivers in high-volume offenses** carry the highest floors in PPR.
- **Quarterbacks who run** have the highest ceilings and the earliest declines;
  the rushing production is the first thing to go.

## Injuries, without panic

Injury history matters less than the fantasy market prices it, with two
exceptions: **soft-tissue injuries that recur**, and **injuries to running backs
past 26**. Everything else — a single ACL, a broken bone, a one-off — has been a
buying opportunity more often than not, because the market discounts the whole
career for one season of risk.

## An honest limit

This article is a set of priors, not projections. Nothing here should be used as
evidence about a specific player without checking that player's actual current
usage. When the loop writes about a named player, it must cite a news item; when
it writes about a *type* of player, articles like this one are the reasoning it
is drawing on.
