The single largest difference between redraft and dynasty is that **you are
pricing a stream of future seasons, not one**. That reframes almost every
decision, and it makes two variables dominant: how old a player is, and what
position they play.

## The rough shape of each position's curve

These are heuristics the dynasty community converges on, not laws. Treat them as
priors you update, not as reasons to ignore what you can see.

| Position | Typically peaks | Falls off | Practical consequence                            |
| -------- | --------------- | --------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| QB       | late 20s        | mid 30s   | The longest useful life of any position           |
| RB       | 23–26           | ~27–28    | The steepest cliff; buy late, sell early          |
| WR       | 25–28           | ~30       | The most stable core asset                        |
| TE       | 26–29           | ~31       | Slow to start, long plateau                       |

Two consequences fall straight out of this table:

1. **Running backs are depreciating assets.** The window where an RB is both
   productive and tradeable is short, and it closes before the production does.
   Managers who wait for the decline to show up in the box score sell into a
   market that has already repriced.
2. **Wide receivers are the ballast.** A young alpha WR holds value across more
   seasons than anything except a young quarterback, which is why the trade cost
   for one rarely looks "fair" in redraft terms.

## Roster shape beats roster quality

A common first-year mistake is optimizing for the best starting lineup this
season and ending up with nine players who all age out together. What you want
instead is a **staggered roster**: some assets peaking now, some peaking in two
years, and a bench that is not full of players you would never start.

Three questions worth asking about any roster:

- **What is the average age of my starters, by position?** If your RBs and WRs
  are all 27+, you are a contender whether or not you planned to be.
- **How many of my bench spots are doing work?** A bench full of aging depth is
  a bench doing nothing. Bench spots are where future value is stored.
- **Am I paying for the same window twice?** Two 29-year-old RBs is not
  diversification; it is one bet made twice.

## Superflex changes the arithmetic

If your league starts a second quarterback — as this one does — QB scarcity
becomes the defining feature of the format. In a 12-team superflex league up to
24 quarterbacks start each week, and there are not 24 good ones. The effects:

- A startable QB is worth substantially more than the same-ranked RB or WR.
- Rostering only one QB is a structural weakness, not a stylistic choice.
- Young quarterbacks on rookie contracts are the most expensive assets in the
  format, because they combine scarcity with the longest age curve.

The corollary is that in superflex you can afford to be patient everywhere else.
Fixing the QB room first is almost always the highest-leverage move available.

## What to do with this

- Sort your roster by age before you sort it by rank. The picture changes.
- Prefer the younger player when two are close — the market will pay you for
  the age later even if the production never separates them.
- Do not roster a running back you would not trade. If you would not sell, ask
  why you would keep the risk.
